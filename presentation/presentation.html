A Five-Pillar Framework for India's Strategic Compute and AI Infrastructure
Dr. Madhavi Dhingra, Adarsh Singh, Parikshit Singh Bais, Anamidi Shrivastava, Sourav
Department of Computer Science and Engineering, Amity School of Engineering and Technology, Gwalior
Track: AI Infrastructure, Semiconductor Sovereignty and Strategic Computing
Click through the timeline. Every strategic technology has split the world into those who built it and those who bought it. Nuclear weapons gave deterrence and a voice in world order. Jet fighters and engines gave air power, and the engine is still the hardest part to master. Rockets gave launch, navigation and surveillance. 5G and 6G decide who controls the standards and networks everyone else runs on. AI is the next one on this line. India has been here before: it built its nuclear and space programmes while others restricted access. AI is the next test, and unlike the others it needs physical infrastructure at enormous scale. That is what this paper measures.

The pattern
A handful of countries got there first, wrote the rules and became powers. Everyone else became a buyer.
India's position
India built its nuclear and space programmes against restrictions. AI is the next test, and it needs physical infrastructure at national scale.
AI is not just software. A chip needs electricity, water, and a supply chain that starts in the Netherlands, runs through Taiwan, and ends in a data centre. India's plan counts GPUs; we asked what those GPUs physically require. The 500,000 figure is a scenario, not a forecast.

Existing studies each cover one piece. The paper calls this disciplinary siloing and lists five specific gaps, RG-01 to RG-05. Safe claim: none of the studies we reviewed combines GPU targets, power, cooling and chip supply in one model. Do not say first ever.
The gap

Each field sees one piece of the chain

Semiconductor analysts

Study who makes the chips and how concentrated supply is.
Ignore how much power a data centre needs.

Power-grid researchers

Study electricity demand, grid flows and stability.
Ignore chip packaging and memory limits.

Policy writers

Set missions, targets and subsidies.
Never count the physical resources a target implies.

One line per pillar. Give one loop as the example: Loop A, the power-compute deadlock. You cannot install GPUs without power, and you cannot get power without proving you will use it. These loops are why the pillars cannot be fixed one at a time.

Loop A

Power-Compute deadlock

No GPUs without power, no power without proof of use.

Loop B

Packaging-Memory co-constraint

One export rule can cut both at once.

Loop C

Data-Connectivity tension

Localisation raises cost and deters investors.

Loop D

Talent-Sovereignty paradox

4.3 million engineers, many in foreign GCCs.

Click through the layers. Start with a number, how many GPUs. Each draws 700 watts, but the server around it makes it 1.82 times that. 500,000 GPUs need 637 megawatts. The building's cooling overhead lifts that to 891.8 megawatts. In parallel the same number drives wafers, packaging and memory. Every number on this slide is computed by code you can re-run.

India is hot and water-stressed. Direct-to-chip liquid cooling cuts the water by 94.6 percent and saves 172 megawatts. This is the cheapest policy lever in our study. Immersion is better on paper but harder to deploy, so D2C is the practical balance.

Logic of the tiers: do what you control first (power, cooling, location), then build with partners (packaging), and manage what you cannot control (foundry, memory). The investment figures are our proposed planning envelopes, not government budgets. Say that out loud.
What India should do

Control, partner, manage: three tiers of action

Tier 1

Build domestically

2026 to 2028
  • Cap Mumbai share below 35% by 2028
  • Direct-to-chip cooling as a subsidy condition
  • 9-month approval window for substations of 50 MW and above
  • AI Power Corridors (5 GW)
Tier 2

Develop via partnerships

2028 to 2031
  • Advanced-packaging joint ventures to reduce CoWoS dependence
  • Chiplet consortium
  • Round-the-clock renewables and small modular reactors
Tier 3

Manage external dependence

2031 to 2035
  • Multi-year wafer reservations (about 1.6% of TSMC output)
  • HBM4 advance-purchase agreements
  • Export-control contingency playbook

Backup 7

Limitations

  1. Fixed 80% utilisation. Real loads vary through the day, with peaks of 20 to 40% above average.
  2. Defect density is a published typical value. Moving it from 0.06 to 0.12 changes wafer needs by -14.9% to +33.3%.
  3. Export rules and diplomacy sit outside the model. EAR and iCET are not represented.
  4. Capacity denominators are market estimates. 780,000 wafers, 450,000 CoWoS and 60 million HBM stacks shift every year.
  5. The model counts GPUs, not performance. Newer chips do more work per watt.
  6. Baseline uses H100-class numbers. Blackwell-class cases appear only in the high variant.
