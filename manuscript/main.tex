\begin{abstract}
National artificial intelligence (AI) strategies increasingly treat high-performance compute accelerators as primary instruments of technological sovereignty. However, compute scaling is physically bounded by electricity transmission, cooling water, semiconductor lithography, advanced packaging, and geopolitically concentrated supply chains. This paper develops an evidence-backed Five-Pillar Conceptual Framework integrated with an auditable, six-layer Demand-Cascade Model to evaluate India's AI infrastructure trajectory across 200,000, 500,000, and 1,000,000 graphics processing unit (GPU) deployment scenarios. Parameterized from empirical data centre audits, foundry specifications, and Central Electricity Authority telemetry, the model translates accelerator targets into peak electrical power, annual energy, cooling water withdrawal, front-end silicon wafer starts, 2.5D interposer packaging allocations, and High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM) stacks. For a baseline 500,000-GPU deployment, the model projects a peak facility power demand of 891.8~MW---equivalent to 56.62\% of India's current operational colocation data centre capacity---and an annual consumption of 6.25~TWh ($4.475\text{ Mt CO}_2$). Crucially, the empirical results refute the conventional hypothesis that front-end semiconductor fabrication capacity is the binding physical chokepoint: required wafer starts represent merely 1.60\% of TSMC's annual sub-5nm output, whereas 2.5D advanced packaging (CoWoS) consumes 7.41\% of total worldwide capacity ($4.6\times$ higher intensity) and HBM3e absorbs 6.67\% of global supply. Consequently, policy planning must invert standard priorities, subordinating premature domestic leading-edge fab construction to high-voltage power transmission corridors, geographical spatial diversification, and bilateral advanced packaging and memory partnerships.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Artificial intelligence (AI) has transitioned from an algorithmic discipline to an ultra-compute-intensive industrial apparatus governed by capital expenditure, high-voltage power grids, and physical microelectronic supply lines. Across leading economies, access to high-density compute clusters is viewed as indispensable for national security, economic productivity, and strategic autonomy \cite{akimitsu2026chokepoints}. In India, this ambition is codified in the statutory authorization of the IndiaAI Mission with a financial outlay of \text{Rs.}~10,371.92 crore ($\sim$\$1.25 billion USD) \cite{meity2024indiaai}, complemented by the India Semiconductor Mission (Semicon India 2.0) with an expanded budgetary horizon of \text{Rs.}~1,27,500 crore \cite{ism2025semicon}. Policy pronouncements frequently articulate deployment goals scaling from an initial subsidized tender of 10,000 graphics processing units (GPUs) toward national fleets exceeding 100,000 to 500,000 accelerators by 2030.

However, prevailing policy discourse frequently decouples accelerator procurement from the physical and industrial systems required to sustain compute operations. Compute does not exist in the ether: an enterprise accelerator cluster demands continuous megawatt-scale electrical power, high-voltage substation interconnects, gigalitres of cooling water under tropical ambient temperatures, and complex supply chains spanning lithography, advanced packaging, and high-bandwidth memory (HBM). Without rigorous physical co-planning, fiscal subsidies risk procuring silicon that sits idle behind multi-year transmission queues or causes severe municipal resource conflicts.

Furthermore, middle-power economies face what scholars term the ``N-2 Problem'' in AI sovereignty \cite{portulans2026sovereignty}: while the United States and China possess the domestic capital and market scale to pursue full-stack technology autonomy, non-superpower nations ($N-2$) cannot realistically build commercial leading-edge sub-3nm foundries ($>\$20$ billion capital expenditure per fab) without facing crippling under-utilization. Attempting to copy superpower autarky policies results in severe resource misallocations.

To address these vulnerabilities, this paper introduces an integrated Five-Pillar Conceptual Framework (Fig.~1) and formulates an auditable, reproducible six-layer Demand-Cascade Model. By mapping exogenous accelerator targets (200k, 500k, 1M GPUs) through empirical physical equations, we quantify the boundary constraints governing India's AI infrastructure trajectory and identify where the true structural chokepoints reside.

\section{Literature Review and Research Gap}
Existing scholarship on AI infrastructure remains fundamentally bifurcated across disconnected technical domains:

\subsection{Data Centre Thermodynamics and Grid Scaling}
Shehabi et al.~\cite{lbnl2024datacenter} establish empirical power profiles for hyperscale accelerator architectures, documenting that server-level host overheads (CPUs, network interface cards, cooling fans) multiply raw chip thermal design power (TDP) by a factor of 1.6 to 2.1. The International Energy Agency (IEA)~\cite{iea2024aienergy} estimates that global data centre electricity demand could exceed 1,000~TWh by 2026, driven primarily by generative AI workloads. Kaufmann et al.~\cite{kaufmann2025powergrid} evaluate transmission congestion, highlighting that multi-hundred-megawatt compute clusters create local substation bottlenecks that cannot be resolved through generic generation capacity alone. In India, industry censuses by JLL~\cite{jll2026indiadc} and CBRE~\cite{cbre2026indiadc} reveal an operational colocation capacity of 1,575~MW in mid-2026, but document an acute spatial vulnerability: 52.4\% of national capacity is concentrated in Mumbai, where grid interconnection queues now extend to 18--36 months under Central Electricity Authority (CEA) guidelines~\cite{cea2026transmission}.

\subsection{Semiconductor Geopolitics and Foundry Chokepoints}
Econometric modeling by Akimitsu~\cite{akimitsu2026chokepoints} demonstrates that upstream AI hardware inputs exhibit extreme market concentration with Herfindahl-Hirschman Index (HHI) values exceeding 7,000 to 10,000 (ASML EUV scanners: 10,000; Refined gallium: 9,804; TSMC CoWoS advanced packaging: 8,100). Over 90\% of commercial sub-5nm logic fabrication resides in Taiwan under TSMC~\cite{tsmc2025annual}. Furthermore, telemetry from SemiAnalysis~\cite{semianalysis2025chokepoints} and TrendForce~\cite{trendforce2026foundry} highlights that the primary hardware bottleneck for modern AI accelerators is not raw wafer lithography, but Chip-on-Wafer-on-Substrate (CoWoS) 2.5D advanced packaging. Similarly, Counterpoint Research~\cite{counterpoint2025hbm} and TrendForce~\cite{trendforce2026hbm} demonstrate that global HBM3e supply is concentrated in a tight triopoly between SK Hynix, Samsung, and Micron. In upstream critical minerals, the USGS~\cite{usgs2025gallium} notes that China controls 98\% of global refined gallium production.

\subsection{The Five Core Research Gaps}
Despite these valuable domain studies, five critical research deficits remain unaddressed (Table~\ref{tab:prior_art}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{Disciplinary Siloing (Gap RG-01):} Microelectronics analysts study fabs in isolation, energy researchers study grid loads without chip yields, and governance scholars omit physical resource accounting.
\item \textit{Absence of a Bottom-Up Demand Cascade (Gap RG-02):} No published study propagates scaled GPU deployments (200k, 500k, 1M GPUs) bottom-up from chip TDP through server nodes, facility PUE, wafer geometry, and packaging interposers.
\item \textit{The Silicon Wafer Fallacy (Gap RG-03):} Mainstream policy discourse assumes that domestic sub-5nm fab capacity is India's binding constraint, without testing whether wafer volume or advanced packaging actually limits deployment.
\item \textit{Tropical Climate Cooling Penalty (Gap RG-04):} Global models assume temperate datacenter baselines (PUE 1.15--1.20) and ignore extreme South Asian summer heatwaves ($>42^\circ\text{C}$), which spike PUE and evaporative water loss.
\item \textit{Operationalizing Coordinated Specialization (Gap RG-05):} NITI Aayog's doctrine of ``Indispensable, Not Imitative'' lacks a quantitative intervention matrix distinguishing domestic build, strategic alliances, and managed external dependency.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Demand-Cascade Model Formulation}
The Demand-Cascade Model maps an exogenous target of $N$ accelerators through six deterministic layers:

\subsection{Layer 1: IT Electrical Load ($P_{\text{IT}}$)}
Active IT power accounts for accelerator board TDP ($P_{\text{gpu}}$) and server chassis overhead ($R_{\text{host}} = 1.82$), incorporating dual host CPUs, DDR5 memory, 3.2~Tbps InfiniBand/RoCE network cards, and PCIe switching fabrics \cite{nvidia2024h100}:
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{IT}} = \frac{N \cdot P_{\text{gpu}} \cdot R_{\text{host}}}{10^6} \quad [\text{MW}]
\label{eq:it_load}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layer 2: Facility Peak Power and Annual Energy}
Facility grid interconnection capacity incorporates Power Usage Effectiveness ($\text{PUE} = 1.40$ baseline tropical average):
\begin{equation}
P_{\text{facility}} = P_{\text{IT}} \cdot \text{PUE} \quad [\text{MW}]
\label{eq:facility_power}
\end{equation}
Annual electrical energy consumption ($E_{\text{annual}}$ in TWh) is a function of cluster capacity utilization factor $U = 0.80$ across 8,760 operating hours:
\begin{equation}
E_{\text{annual}} = \frac{P_{\text{facility}} \cdot 8760 \cdot U}{10^6} \quad [\text{TWh}]
\label{eq:energy}
\end{equation}
Operational greenhouse gas emissions reflect the Central Electricity Authority (CEA) grid emission factor $\text{EF}_{\text{grid}} = 0.716\text{ tCO}_2/\text{MWh}$ \cite{cea2026transmission}:
\begin{equation}
\text{Emissions}_{\text{annual}} = E_{\text{annual}} \cdot \text{EF}_{\text{grid}} \quad [\text{Million tonnes CO}_2]
\label{eq:carbon}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layer 3: Thermal Water Withdrawal and Equipment Racks}
Annual evaporative cooling water consumption reflects water intensity $I_{\text{water}}$ (L/kWh) \cite{iea2024aienergy}:
\begin{equation}
W_{\text{annual}} = \frac{E_{\text{annual}} \cdot 10^9 \cdot I_{\text{water}}}{10^6} \quad [\text{ML}]
\label{eq:water}
\end{equation}
Physical rack footprint ($N_{\text{rack}}$) is determined by target rack power density ($D_{\text{rack}} = 60\text{ kW/rack}$):
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{rack}} = \left\lceil \frac{P_{\text{IT}} \cdot 10^{3}}{D_{\text{rack}}} \right\rceil
\label{eq:racks}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layer 4: Semiconductor Silicon Wafer Starts}
Gross dies per 300~mm wafer ($N_{\text{gross}}$) are calculated from radius $r = 150$~mm and die area $A_{\text{die}} = 814\text{ mm}^2$ (NVIDIA GH100 reticle limit) via the edge-loss equation:
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{gross}} = \left\lfloor \frac{\pi r^2}{A_{\text{die}}} - \frac{2 \pi r}{\sqrt{2 A_{\text{die}}}} \right\rfloor = 63
\label{eq:gross_dies}
\end{equation}
Analytical defect yield is formulated via the Murphy model with defect density $D_0 = 0.08\text{ cm}^{-2}$:
\begin{equation}
Y_{\text{Murphy}} = \left( \frac{1 - e^{-D_0 A_{\text{die}}}}{D_0 A_{\text{die}}} \right)^2 \approx 0.540
\label{eq:murphy}
\end{equation}
Accounting for industrial binning and core harvesting, net functional yield reaches $Y = 0.65$, giving $N_{\text{good}} = \lfloor 63 \cdot 0.65 \rfloor = 40$ good dies per processed wafer. Total required 300~mm wafer starts are:
\begin{equation}
W_{\text{starts}} = \left\lceil \frac{N}{N_{\text{good}}} \right\rceil
\label{eq:wafer_starts}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Layers 5 and 6: 2.5D Packaging and HBM Memory}
Total 300~mm silicon interposer wafers required for CoWoS packaging ($W_{\text{CoWoS}}$) are derived from finished packages per interposer wafer $C_{\text{CoWoS}} = 15$:
\begin{equation}
W_{\text{CoWoS}} = \left\lceil \frac{N}{C_{\text{CoWoS}}} \right\rceil
\label{eq:cowos}
\end{equation}
Total HBM3e memory stacks ($N_{\text{HBM}}$) scale with stack density ($N_{\text{stacks}} = 8$ per package):
\begin{equation}
N_{\text{HBM}} = N \cdot N_{\text{stacks}}
\label{eq:hbm}
\end{equation}

\section{Empirical Results and Discussion}

\subsection{Scenario Scaling Analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:scenarios} summarizes model outputs across all three deployment targets. In the baseline 500,000-GPU scenario, active IT load reaches 637.0~MW. Under tropical climatic conditions (PUE = 1.40), peak facility power escalates to \textbf{891.8~MW}. This represents \textbf{56.62\% of India's entire operational colocation data centre capacity} (1,575~MW in mid-2026 \cite{jll2026indiadc}). At 80\% utilization, annual electricity consumption totals \textbf{6.25~TWh/year} (generating $4.475\text{ Mt CO}_2$), while evaporative water dissipation reaches \textbf{11,249.5~ML/year} (11.25 million cubic meters).

\subsection{Refuting the ``Silicon Wafer'' Hypothesis}
A foundational finding of this research is the empirical refutation of the widespread policy belief that domestic front-end semiconductor fabrication capacity is the binding physical chokepoint for sovereign AI compute (Fig.~\ref{fig:inversion}):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Sub-5nm Logic Wafer Demand:} Deploying 500,000 GPUs requires exactly \textbf{12,500 300~mm wafer starts}.
\item \textit{Global Foundry Context:} TSMC's sub-5nm manufacturing capacity exceeds 65,000 wafer starts per month, or $\sim$\textbf{780,000 wafers annually} \cite{tsmc2025annual}.
\item \textit{Relative Scarcity:} India's 500k-GPU requirement consumes \textbf{merely 1.60\% of annual global sub-5nm wafer output} (and only 3.21\% at 1,000,000 GPUs).
\end{itemize}
Thus, raw silicon lithography is easily absorbable by commercial foundry supply lines provided procurement capital is allocated.

\subsection{Identification of the True Structural Chokepoints}
The model demonstrates that the binding physical and microelectronic constraints reside in packaging, memory, and domestic infrastructure (Table~\ref{tab:chokepoints}):
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textit{2.5D Advanced Packaging (CoWoS):} Packaging 500,000 GPUs requires \textbf{33,334 CoWoS interposer wafers}. With global CoWoS capacity bounded at $\sim$450,000 wafers/year \cite{trendforce2026foundry}, India absorbs \textbf{7.41\% of total worldwide packaging output}. This represents a bottleneck intensity \textbf{4.6 times higher} than front-end wafer fabrication.
\item \textit{High-Bandwidth Memory (HBM3e):} Deploying 500,000 accelerators requires \textbf{4,000,000 HBM stacks} ($\mathbf{6.67\%}$ of annual global output), where India possesses zero domestic fabrication or packaging capability.
\item \textit{Spatial Transmission Congestion:} Supplying 891.8~MW into Mumbai (which holds 52.4\% of existing data centres) requires 18 dedicated 50~MW CEA substations \cite{cea2026transmission}, triggering grid saturation and 24--36 month interconnection delays.
\item \textit{Municipal Water Stress:} Consuming 11.25 billion litres/year under conventional wet cooling creates direct conflict with urban drinking water in water-stressed corridors.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Sensitivity and Monte Carlo Uncertainty Analysis}
Tornado analysis (Fig.~\ref{fig:sensitivity}) and Monte Carlo simulations (1,000 runs) demonstrate that facility power is exceptionally sensitive to PUE and GPU TDP: transitioning from tropical air cooling (PUE = 1.55) to D2C liquid cooling (PUE = 1.18) \textbf{saves 235.7~MW of peak grid demand} at 500k scale. \section{Strategic Roadmap and Policy Implications}

\subsection{Documented Priority Inversion: Debunking ``Foundry-First''}
Standard policy discourse advocates spending \$15--\$20 billion to build a domestic sub-5nm logic foundry. Our cascade model proves this is a strategic misallocation. A 500k-GPU sovereign cluster consumes only 12,500 wafers/year---less than 2\% of the annual volume needed to economically sustain a modern gigafab. Furthermore, domestic logic dies produce zero operational accelerators without 2.5D packaging, HBM, and 891.8~MW of substation power.

The framework directs an \textbf{immediate priority inversion}:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Old Priority:} Sub-5nm logic fabrication megaprojects (\$15B+ capital risk, 7--10 year lead time).
\item \textit{Inverted New Priority:} High-voltage Green AI transmission corridors, spatial decentralization to power-surplus states, and bilateral packaging/memory joint ventures.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Three-Tier Strategic Intervention Matrix}
Interventions are categorized into three operational tiers based on multi-criteria feasibility and sovereignty leverage:

\subsubsection{Tier 1: Build Domestically (2026--2028)}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Spatial Diversification:} Mandate 15--20\% capital rebates directing greenfield AI clusters away from Mumbai into Gujarat, Rajasthan, Odisha, and Madhya Pradesh, reducing Mumbai's concentration below 35\%.
\item \textit{Liquid Cooling Mandate:} Condition IndiaAI compute subsidies on $\text{PUE} \le 1.25$ and closed-loop dry heat rejection, eliminating 11 billion litres/year of municipal water loss.
\item \textit{Fast-Track Grid Substations:} Amend Central Electricity Authority regulations to create a dedicated 9-month approval window for 400/220~kV substations serving compute clusters $\ge 50$~MW.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Tier 2: Partner Strategically (2028--2031)}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Advanced Packaging JVs:} Expand approved domestic ATMP/OSAT facilities (Tata Electronics, Micron) toward 2.5D silicon and glass interposers via joint ventures with Taiwanese and Japanese OSATs under Semicon 2.0.
\item \textit{Critical Mineral Refining:} Scale CSIR-IMMT technology to extract refined gallium from domestic bauxite refinery residue (red mud) at NALCO and Hindalco, producing 50 metric tons/year of high-purity gallium.
\item \textit{Sovereign Chiplet Consortium:} Mobilize India's 120,000 VLSI design engineers into an open-standard (UCIe) consortium to tape out domain-specific RISC-V matrix acceleration chiplets on mature foundry nodes.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Tier 3: Manage External Dependencies (2031--2035)}
\begin{itemize}
\item Secure multi-year sovereign foundry capacity reservation agreements at TSMC and bilateral memory supply treaties with South Korea, leveraging India's large consumer market as strategic trade offsets.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion and Limitations}
This paper presents the first reproducible, multi-layer demand-cascade framework linking national AI accelerator targets directly to electric transmission, cooling thermodynamics, and semiconductor supply chains. The empirical findings prove that India's sovereign compute bottleneck is not front-end lithography volume, but 2.5D CoWoS packaging, HBM allocation, and metropolitan grid congestion. By prioritizing transmission corridors, spatial dispersion, and packaging alliances, India can deploy resilient sovereign AI infrastructure without falling victim to capital misallocations.

\textit{Limitations:} The model assumes static annual cluster utilization (80\%) without dynamic nodal power dispatch, and relies on public foundry defect density disclosures. Future research will integrate real-time AC power-flow simulations and dynamic diurnal inference scaling.
